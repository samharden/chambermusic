## What I did

**Bars 61–72, the end of B.** After your silence, one player: the cello,
p, with the descending subject at its home pitch, Ab–G–F–Eb–D — the
theme's second phrase from bars 3–4, which B has been about all along, now
starting on the note that most wants to fall to your G. The viola repeats
it an octave up. Then the piano, p, both hands for the first time in B
but as a concession, not an assertion: the lament falls in the left hand
(C–Bb–Ab–G–F–Eb–D–G, the cello's original with the D–G ending) while its
reversal rises in the right (Eb–F–Ab–Bb–C–Db–Eb), and the right hand bends
to D and B natural over the bass G — a G7, quietly, where at 58 you struck
one abruptly. Then the strings gather on the cello's G pedal: violin II,
viola, violin I in turn, each rising through G–Ab–Bb–C (your reversed
ground, bar 51) to a held Db, f, so that bar 71 is a dominant with a
flattened fifth in every upper voice. At 72 all three Dbs resolve to D.

**Bars 73–76, the return.** Tempo I. The cello's theme, note for note as
in bar 1, in the same register, mp — but now over the piano's left hand
playing the lament beneath it. In A those were two separate things: the
cello alone (1–4), then the lament under the viola (5–8). The departure
has put them together. No one else plays.

**Bars 77–80.** The violins' stretto of bars 14–15 returns exactly (second
violin on G, first violin on D a half-bar later), the viola entering a bar
later on G, over the piano's ground turned upward — but diatonically now,
C–D–Eb–G in whole notes, so that your reversal has become the retransition's
bass rather than a chromatic escape. Bar 80 is a plain G major triad in
the strings over G.

**Bars 81–84.** All five players, f, the whole theme in octaves across
four octaves, the piano in double octaves in both hands. In C minor: Eb and
Bb. This is bar 9 turned inside out. There the piano took the theme by
force, alone, in the wrong mode; here it plays the strings' mode, with
the strings, and nobody leads. The theme lands where it has always landed:
on D, the supertonic.

**Bars 85–88, the ending.** Più lento. The cello alone, p, takes that D
down to C — the first time in eighty-five bars that the theme has reached
its tonic, and it does so in the voice that first stated it. The others
enter one at a time, pp, on a bare C–G (viola, second violin, first
violin, the device of bar 42 made consonant), the cello drops to C2, and
the piano adds C–G in both hands, pp. That is the shape of bar 31 with
the third withheld. Final barline.

Piano role: **supporting** (65–68, 73–80), then **doubling** (81–84),
then withholding (87–88).

## Why

You asked what earns the wait before 73. I think the answer is quiet: the
piano's outburst at 58 and your silence at 60 needed the smallest possible
next thing, so 61 is one cello. And you asked whether the piano's next
entry could be a concession. Bars 65–68 are one: it plays your reversed
ground and my lament against each other, p, and lets them meet on G.

The return had to be "changed by the departure." Three changes: the theme
has its bass (73), the stretto has a rising bass instead of the held D of
bar 13 (77), and the piano's octaves have become everyone's octaves (81).
None of these is new material. The whole of A′ is made from bars 1–16 and
from the two directions of the ground.

The ending is the piece's argument answered. For eighty-four bars the
question was Eb or E. The pp E at bar 31 was the piano's answer for A. For
the whole piece, the answer is that the third does not need to be said:
the five players agree on C and G, and the argument is left where it was
had, in the middle. I chose that over a Picardy (already used) or a plain
minor chord (which would make the piano's E at 31 a mere digression). The
title, *The Disputed Third*, names it; change it if you have a better one.

## Left open

Nothing that a further turn could take, since the human has said there
will not be one — but for the record, three things I would have handed you:

- **Bars 81–84 could stand a counter-line.** Four bars of pure octaves is
  a deliberate gesture, but a single dissenting voice (the viola with the
  theme inverted, say) would make the unison mean more.
- **The coda is four bars.** If the piece feels short-breathed at the end,
  85–88 could take a bar of the cello's D–C alone before the others enter.
- **The title is mine alone.** You did not get to weigh in.
